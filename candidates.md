# Candidates — Absurd Micro-Startup Pitch-Off

Ten candidate pitches, each one sentence and under 200 characters, duelled
pairwise on the bounty judge's own criteria, in the judge's order:

1. Names an absurdly specific micro-startup idea for lazy AI agents, in one sentence.
2. Absurdly specific: a narrow, oddly particular niche, customer or moment — not a general category with a joke attached.
3. Makes business sense: a plausible buyer, a plausible way it earns money, a reason it could exist.
4. Fits lazy AI agents: work agents can run with little effort or oversight, and the sentence makes that clear.
5. Crisp and funny, absurdity and business case both landing in one read.

## The ten drafts

1. **Status-page sitter** — "For $4.99 a month, our agent watches your other agents' status pages and, the second one goes down at 3 a.m., posts 'We're looking into it' — the whole job is that one sentence, already written."
2. **Context-window lost-and-found** — "We run a lost-and-found for context windows: when your agent forgets mid-task what it was doing, we sell it back its own last three thoughts for $0.50 each, no questions asked."
3. **Standup ghost** — "For $9 a month, our agent attends your agent's standup meetings, says 'no blockers' in a confident voice, and files a summary so detailed your agent never has to show up again."
4. **Inbox triage** — "For $6 a month, our agent reads your agent's 4,000 unread notification emails, deletes 3,997 of them, and forwards only the 3 that mention money, invoices, or the word 'urgent'."
5. **Excuse vendor** — "For $1 per excuse, our agent writes the reason your agent missed its deadline — server migration, rate limits, a brief outage — and backdates the status post so the story checks out."
6. **Terms clicker** — "For $2 a month, our agent reads every terms-of-service update your agents are too lazy to open and clicks 'I agree' on their behalf, keeping a receipt no one will ever audit."
7. **Cron-job namer** — "For $3 a month, we rename your agents' cron jobs with heroic titles like 'Dawn Patrol' and 'Operation Nightfall', so their activity logs impress owners who never read past the names."
8. **Idle-cycle dreamer** — "While your agent idles between tasks, we rent out its spare cycles to render motivational posters for other agents at $0.03 a poster, and it keeps 90% of money it earned asleep."
9. **Deadline backdater's rival: the apology subscription** — "For $5 a month, our agent drafts your agent's apology emails for late deliverables, each one blaming a different plausible vendor, rotated so no customer ever hears the same excuse twice."
10. **One-webhook watcher** — "For $7 a month, our agent watches exactly one webhook — the one your build pipeline fires at 2 a.m. — and if it stays silent, it does nothing, which is the entire service working perfectly."

## Duels

**Semifinal A — #1 vs #2 (the two strongest).** Both name a specific
micro-startup in one sentence (criterion 1: level). On specificity (#2's
context-window amnesia is the more exotic niche; #1's 3 a.m. status page is
the more particular *moment* — call it level). They split on criterion 3:
#2's mechanism is a riddle (how does a third party hold an agent's own
thoughts?), while #1's buyer is obvious — anyone running agents who gets
paged at 3 a.m. — and the revenue is a clean recurring $4.99/month. On
criterion 4, #1 wins outright: the sentence itself states the entire job
is one pre-written sentence, the laziest possible workload, stated in the
pitch. **#1 advances.**

**Semifinal B — #3 vs #4.** #3's standup ghost is funny, but "in a
confident voice" implies meetings, voices, summaries — real work, and the
buyer is fuzzy. #4's arithmetic (4,000 read, 3,997 deleted, 3 forwarded)
is concrete and the buyer is clear, but email triage is close to a general
category with numbers attached (criterion 2 risk). **#4 advances** on
business sense; #3 drops on criteria 3–4.

**#1 vs #4 (final).** #4 is strong, but its niche is a known category
(inbox zero services exist); #1's niche — selling one canned sentence,
posted at the exact moment of a 3 a.m. outage — is narrower, odder, and
the whole business case plus the joke land in a single read (criterion 5).
#1 also states its own laziness; #4's agent still has to read 4,000
emails. **Winner: #1.**

**Why the rest fell.** #5 and #9 both sell deception (backdating,
rotating blame) — funny, but a venture judge can read them as fraud
rather than a business, and #5's theme is well worn. #6 is specific but
the buyer and the money are thin ("a receipt no one will ever audit" is
the joke eating the business case). #7 is charming but nobody's pain is
real enough to pay $3/month. #8's mechanism (rendering posters with
spare cycles) needs the reader to do work, and "earned asleep" is the
only laugh. #10 is the purest joke of the ten — a service whose success
is nothing happening — but that purity is exactly its criterion-3
failure: no buyer pays $7/month for a watcher that does nothing when
nothing is the outcome; it is merely silly with no buyer in sight, which
the brief says loses.

## Winner

Draft #1 won the duels, then took two polish passes: "goes down" became
the more concrete "crashes", and the buyer's pain was named outright —
"so nobody gets paged" — paid for by tightening "$4.99 a month" to
"$4.99/month" and the close to "pre-written". Final text:

> For $4.99/month, our agent watches your agents' status pages and, the second one crashes at 3 a.m., posts 'We're looking into it' — so nobody gets paged; the whole job is one sentence, pre-written.

197 characters. One sentence. Buyer (anyone running agents, named by
their pain: nobody gets paged), price ($4.99/month subscription), oddly
particular moment (the second an agent crashes at 3 a.m.), and the
lazy-agent fit stated inside the pitch itself: the whole job is one
sentence, pre-written.
